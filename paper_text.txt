 A B S T R A C T
With the rapid growth of electric vehicles and renewable energy storage systems, lithium-ion batteries have 
become a cornerstone of modern energy infrastructure, making accurate and trustworthy remaining useful life 
(RUL) prediction crucial for operational safety. However, most existing data-driven models predominantly 
focus on deterministic point estimates while overlooking the underlying prediction uncertainty, which 
severely undermines the trustworthiness of RUL predictions in safety-critical applications. This paper proposes 
a Sparsely-gated Mixture of Experts Long Short-Term Memory (SMoE-LSTM) model for trustworthy RUL 
prediction. The model integrates a sparsely gated mixture of experts with LSTM networks to adaptively 
capture complex, multi-stage degradation dynamics. The model further incorporates deep evidential regression. 
This allows for the simultaneous output of RUL estimates and decoupled uncertainties in a single forward 
propagation. Experimental validation on the MIT-Stanford dataset demonstrates that the proposed method 
achieves an average RMSE of 16.11 and an R2 of 0.9821, outperforming baseline methods in both accuracy and 
uncertainty calibration. Moreover, its single-pass inference design provides substantially higher computational 
efficiency than MC Dropout and ensemble methods, making it more suitable for real-time battery management 
systems.
1. Introduction
Amid global efforts to achieve carbon neutrality and transform 
energy systems, renewable energy-dominated systems and low-carbon 
transportation are experiencing unprecedented growth [1,2]. Lithium-
ion batteries have become a key energy storage solution supporting 
this transformation due to their high energy density, long cycle life, 
and environmental friendliness [3,4]. These batteries are widely used 
in core energy systems of advanced facilities, such as energy storage 
power stations [5], electric vehicles [6], electric vertical take-off and 
landing aircraft [7], and aerospace systems [8]. However, the stochas-
tic nature of battery degradation and complex operating conditions 
render batteries susceptible to safety risks, such as capacity fade and 
thermal runaway [9–11]. These uncertainties pose severe challenges to 
the trustworthiness of battery management systems (BMS). Therefore, 
remaining useful life (RUL) prediction methodologies must evolve from 
seeking mere accuracy to achieving quantifiable trustworthiness. A 
trustworthy RUL prediction requires not only precise estimations but 
also the capability to alert users to potential risks through predic-
tive uncertainty, thereby enabling RUL prediction-based maintenance 
strategies.

Existing methods for predicting the RUL of lithium-ion batteries 
are generally divided into two categories [12]: model-driven and data-
driven approaches. Model-driven methods establish physical models 
to describe the battery degradation process [13], with the goal of 
constructing relationships between observable parameters and aging 
indicators. These can be further classified into empirical models and 
physical models. Empirical models are based on mathematical repre-
sentations of capacity fade trajectories, such as polynomial, double-
exponential, and logarithmic models [14–16]. Through data fitting, 
they map cycle counts to maximum available capacity, enabling RUL 
estimation and projection of future degradation trends. Physical mod-
els capture complex electrochemical reactions and aging mechanisms, 
making them suitable for adaptive modeling under varying operating 
conditions [17–19]. However, model-driven approaches depend on 
accurate physicochemical parameters, which are often difficult to ob-
tain, and typically involve complex equations and high computational 
costs. In contrast, data-driven methods can capture complex nonlin-
ear degradation patterns directly from historical data without relying 
on detailed physicochemical parameters [20–22]. Typical approaches

and RUL. Park et al. [23] proposed a hybrid deep learning model that 
integrates LSTM attention mechanisms with MLP to improve prediction 
accuracy by focusing on both statistical and domain-specific features. 
Chen et al. [24] developed TOP-Net, a parallel hybrid network com-

prediction for lithium-ion batteries under diverse operating conditions.
Among these methods, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) net-
work has been adopted extensively in the field of battery RUL pre-
diction, owing to its capacity to capture long-term dependencies in 
capacity fade [25–27]. Zraibi et al. [28] compared DNN, RNN, GRU, 
and LSTM models, finding that LSTM achieved superior performance 
in battery RUL prediction with lower prediction errors relative to the 
other methods. To improve model robustness and adaptability, the 
Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework has emerged as a promising 
solution [29–31]. Through a gating mechanism, MoE dynamically as-
signs different experts to different degradation patterns, which helps 
reduce modeling bias caused by heterogeneous aging trajectories. Chen 
and Zhou [32] proposed an AttMoE network that integrates attention 
mechanisms with a MoE layer, where multiple specialized experts 
collaboratively enhance the model’s ability to capture diverse degra-
dation patterns, thereby improving the accuracy of RUL prediction. 
Dong et al. [33] proposed LB-DeformNet, which incorporates a Load-
Balancing Restriction MoE to ensure uniform expert activation, thereby 
enhancing model capacity and representation diversity for more robust 
lithium-ion battery RUL prediction. The integration of diverse experts 
enables the capture of a wider spectrum of patterns in the data, 
resulting in enhanced overall performance and more comprehensive 
representations.
However, conventional data-driven methodologies frequently yield 
deterministic point estimates of RUL, whereas real-world battery opera-
tion is characterized by variable conditions and intricate electrochemi-
cal processes. As intuitively illustrated in Fig.  1(a), battery degradation 
is a highly non-linear process where prediction errors are not static; 
instead, they exhibit a cumulative propagation effect due to the re-
cursive nature of RUL estimation. As the aging process evolves, these 
accumulated errors lead to diverging degradation trajectories, making a 
single point estimate inherently insufficient and potentially misleading. 
Fig.  1(b) highlights the critical necessity for trustworthy RUL predic-
tion. In the context of BMS, relying solely on point estimates may lead 
to misguided maintenance decisions and increased costs. Therefore, 
the provision of RUL predictions accompanied by Uncertainty Quan-
tification (UQ) is essential [34]. A trustworthy RUL prediction should 
not only provide an accurate estimate but also deliver well-calibrated 
quantification of its own reliability. This enables decision-makers to 
understand the limitations and risks associated with the prediction, 
thereby supporting well-informed judgments in maintenance planning 
and system management.
Therefore, establishing a trustworthy RUL prediction necessitates 
advanced UQ capabilities, specifically the ability to decompose uncer-
tainty into its constituent sources. The classification of uncertainty is 
typically based on its source, with epistemic uncertainty and aleatoric 
uncertainty being the two primary categories [35]. Epistemic uncer-
tainty, which stems from a lack of knowledge within the model, can 
be attributed to factors such as the lack of training data or the im-
proper configuration of the model architecture. This uncertainty can 
be mitigated through the augmentation of datasets or the refinement of 
the model itself. Conversely, aleatoric uncertainty captures the inherent 
randomness or noise present in the data, which remains unavoidable 
even when employing a perfect model. For a trustworthy prediction, 
the model should distinguish between these two uncertainty sources. 
High aleatoric uncertainty may indicate noisy operating conditions and 
the need for signal filtering. High epistemic uncertainty may indicate 
that the battery is operating in a state that is insufficiently repre-
sented in the training data, suggesting the need for more conservative 
control strategies. This decomposition provides the reason behind an 
unreliable prediction, fundamentally enhancing the transparency and 
trustworthiness of the RUL prediction.
UQ in lithium-ion battery RUL prediction currently relies primarily 
on three methodological approaches: Bayesian neural networks, Gaus-
sian Process Regression (GPR), and ensemble methods [36]. Bayesian 
neural networks characterize epistemic uncertainty at the model pa-
rameter level by imposing prior distributions on network weights 
and transforming them into posterior distributions [37,38]. Zhang 
et al. [39] proposed a Bayesian hybrid neural network framework 
that uses variational Bayesian inference to output predictive distribu-
tions. This improves the accuracy and stability of RUL prediction and 
provides uncertainty estimates. Ke et al. [40] developed a Bayesian 
neural network using Monte Carlo sampling and variational inference 
to account for prediction uncertainty. Gaussian process regression is 
a non-parametric Bayesian method that defines priors directly in the 
function space and derives complete posterior predictive distributions 
via covariance kernel functions. This method naturally offers built-in 
UQ. Liu et al. [41] proposed an LSTM+GPR hybrid framework that 
uses the empirical mode decomposition to split battery capacity data 
into some Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs) and a residual, LSTM to fit 
the residual for long-term dependence, and GPR to capture IMFs with 
uncertainty. Ensemble methods reduce variance and bias at the system 
level by constructing and combining predictions from multiple het-
erogeneous base learners [42,43]. Epistemic uncertainty is estimated 
by evaluating disagreement among the models. Hu and Wang [44] 
proposed a two-stage ensemble training approach that incorporates 
a cosine annealing strategy to improve stochastic weight averaging, 
along with a quantile regression method to quantify uncertainty in RUL 
prediction, thereby mitigating the overconfidence issue often associated 
with ensemble methods.
While UQ methods for battery RUL prediction have been prelim-
inary investigated, existing approaches exhibit notable limitations re-
garding practical trustworthiness. Bayesian methods incur substantial 
computational overhead due to their reliance on repeated sampling, 
and their theoretical complexity often hinders the effective decompo-
sition of uncertainty in practice. Gaussian process regression is con-
strained by its kernel design, which limits scalability, complicates the 
handling of high-dimensional battery data, and prevents end-to-end 
feature learning [45]. Although ensemble methods can improve pre-
diction robustness, they cannot explicitly distinguish between different 
sources of uncertainty, and their multi-model architecture leads to high 
costs in both training and inference [46]. In contrast, Deep evidential 
regression offers a paradigm shift towards efficient trustworthiness. 
As compared in Fig.  1(c), unlike Bayesian or Ensemble approaches 
that require complex sampling or multiple model runs, deep evidential 
regression models the higher-order evidential distribution to simulta-
neously output RUL prediction and decoupled uncertainty estimates 
in a single forward pass [47,48]. This characteristic perfectly satisfies 
the real-time performance and trustworthiness requirements in RUL 
prediction.
In summary, despite advancements in data-driven methodologies 
and UQ techniques, a critical research gap persists in achieving a syn-
ergistic balance among model adaptiveness, computational efficiency, 
and uncertainty interpretability. Existing UQ frameworks primarily rely 
on static network architectures that struggle to capture the highly het-
erogeneous and multi-stage nature of battery degradation. Meanwhile, 
traditional UQ approaches impose prohibitive computational burdens 
due to repeated sampling or ensemble execution, and frequently fail to 
explicitly decouple aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties [49]. More-
over, most existing methods operate as black box models with limited 
explainability. They provide predictions without revealing why certain 
decisions are made or what factors contribute to the model’s confi-
dence. This lack of transparency further undermines trustworthiness,

mechanism makes it possible to examine which experts are activated 
under different degradation conditions. On the predictive side, the 
explicit decomposition of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties helps 
explain whether low-confidence predictions are mainly associated with 
data noise or limited model knowledge. The primary contributions of 
this study are as follows:
1. A collaborative SMoE-LSTM framework is constructed to ro-
bustly model complex, multi-stage degradation dynamics. By 
adaptively fusing expert knowledge, this architecture enhances 
the model’s generalization capability across diverse operating 
conditions, ensuring prediction reliability
2. A trustworthy UQ mechanism based on deep evidential regres-

3. Comprehensive validation on the MIT-Stanford dataset demon-

between accuracy and efficiency. It significantly improves un-
certainty calibration compared to baseline methods, providing a 
verifiable and transparent solution for trustworthy battery RUL 
prediction.

mean and variance of the target variable. Instead, it employs the 
establishment of a probabilistic model for the parameters of these like-
lihood functions, thereby encapsulating both aleatoric and epistemic 
uncertainty [53].

This theory shifts uncertainty modeling from the weight space to 
the likelihood parameter space, offering a scalable and sampling-free 
alternative for UQ in regression tasks.
3. Methodology
This section delineates a framework for lithium-ion battery RUL 
prediction. The methodology is structured around three core compo-
nents to address the challenges of complex degradation dynamics and 
inherent data variability. First, a rolling sampling strategy is introduced 
to construct sequential training samples and enable dynamic, cycle-by-
cycle RUL estimation. Second, a multi-source feature extraction scheme 
is detailed, encompassing tailored methods for charging, discharging, 
and summary data to comprehensively characterize battery health

3.1. Rolling sampling method for lithium-ion battery RUL
This paper employs a sliding window-based RUL dynamic rolling 
prediction method, as illustrated in Fig.  3, enabling the prediction 
to continuously update as the cycle progresses. During the training 
phase, the complete lifecycle data are utilized. Commencing from the 
initial cycle, a sliding window of a fixed length l traverses the entire 
degradation process. Within each designated window, the extraction of 
features is contingent upon the utilization of data pertaining exclusively 
to the process variables contained within that particular window. The 
RUL label is calculated as the difference between the current cycle 
index and the cycle number at the battery’s end-of-life (EOL). The 
following formula is used to calculate the RUL label: 
RUL𝑛 = EOL − ( 𝑙 + 𝑛) (9)
where 𝑛 denotes the number of window shifts. Each shift yields a 
feature sequence of length 𝑙 and its corresponding RUL label. For a 
battery sample with a total cycle life of 𝑁, this process can generate 
𝑁 − 𝑙 training samples. During actual operation, this method enables 
dynamic rolling output of RUL predictions by continuously collecting 
process data cycle-by-cycle and feeding the window features into the 
model. Furthermore, the method does not impose strict requirements 
on the starting point of the window, allowing it to skip missing cycle 
samples during data acquisition. This characteristic makes it partic-
ularly suitable for application scenarios where initial data may be 
incomplete or collected intermittently, demonstrating strong data fault 
tolerance. Overall, this approach provides a feasible pathway for rolling 
RUL prediction of lithium-ion batteries.
Furthermore, given the raw battery dataset’s notable presence of 
extreme outliers, which have the potential to adversely affect model 
prediction performance, these anomalous values are eliminated prior to 
feature extraction based on the statistical 3𝜎 criterion. Missing values 
are subsequently filled using linear interpolation.

Significant discrepancies in scale and unit of measurement are 
evident among the multi-source features that have been extracted. For 
instance, characteristic values such as voltage, current, capacity, and 
resistance are distributed across disparate numerical ranges, while the 
RUL label predicted by the model, expressed in cycle count, operates

synchronously process all features and labels.
In summary, the selection of this multi-source feature set is not 
merely driven by empirical data variance, but is deeply grounded in 
the fundamental electrochemical mechanisms of battery degradation. 
Specifically, the charging features (𝑉1..6, 𝐼1..6) reflect changes in polar-
ization and electrochemical impedance during repeated fast cycling. 
The IC-based features (𝐹1, 𝐹2) characterize electrode phase-transition 
behavior and are closely related to LAM and LLI. In addition, the 
increase in DC internal resistance (𝐹3) reflects impedance growth, 
while the maximum discharge capacity (𝐹4) indicates the overall health 
state of the battery. Together, these physically meaningful features 
provide a sound basis for subsequent RUL prediction and uncertainty 
quantification.

3.3.2. Overall model structure and evidence layer
To effectively achieve UQ in predicting the RUL of lithium-ion 
batteries, this paper designs and implements a SMoE-LSTM Model. In 
contrast to conventional methodologies, such as MC Dropout or model 
ensemble, SMoE-LSTM Model provides point predictions and epistemic 
uncertainty, while also explicitly quantifying aleatoric uncertainty.
As demonstrated in Fig.  9, the model is predominantly comprised 
of a feature extractor and an evidence layer. Initially, the input battery 
process data is entered into a deep neural network based feature 
extractor, which results in the acquisition of high-dimensional feature 
representations. These features are then passed to the evidence layer, 
which employs fully connected networks to output four parameters 
(𝛾, 𝜆, 𝛼, 𝛽) that collectively define the posterior distribution of the target 
variable, i.e. the NIG distribution. This distribution provides a point 
estimate of the RUL and explicitly quantifies the predictive uncertainty.
To enhance the accuracy and robustness of lithium-ion battery 
RUL prediction, the feature extractor in the proposed model adopts a 
collaborative fusion model of SMoE and LSTM. This design combines 
the advantages of time-series modeling and multi-subspace representa-
tion, enabling effective capture of long-term dependencies and complex 
degradation patterns during battery aging. The resulting high dimen-
sional feature representations, which are subsequently passed to the 
evidence layer, are generated by processing the battery data through 
this feature extractor.
The mathematical modeling details of the evidence layer are also 
illustrated in Fig.  9. In the context of the regression task, it is hy-
pothesized that the target values 𝑦 are drawn from an i.i.d. Gaussian 
observation model 𝑦𝑖 ∼  (𝜇, 𝜎2). In contrast to conventional re-

directly, the deep evidential regression approach considers both the 
mean 𝜇 and the variance 𝜎2 as unknown random variables. The con-

mean, pseudo-count (related to sample size), degrees of freedom, and 
scale, respectively. Each of these parameters has a clear statistical 
interpretation.
In summary, the SMoE-LSTM model achieves intrinsic calibration 
and simultaneous decomposition of uncertainty without relying on 
external sampling or model ensembles. While maintaining high pre-
diction accuracy, the method effectively enhances the reliability and 
confidence of lithium-ion battery RUL prediction, making it suitable for 
practical application in battery management systems. It is noteworthy 
that, although numerous post-processing-based uncertainty calibration 
methods exist in current research, deep evidential regression accom-
plishes intrinsic calibration of predictive uncertainty during model 
training through an adjustable regularization term, thereby eliminating 
the need for additional post-processing or retraining.

4.1. Dataset description
This paper uses a lithium-ion battery dataset that has been collab-
oratively developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and 
Stanford University [58]. The dataset under consideration comprises 
124 commercial LiFePO4 graphite batteries (APR18650M1A) manu-
factured by A123 Systems. Fig.  11 shows the schematic diagram of 
the whole cycle test process of the MIT-Stanford dataset. The batteries 
were subjected to a series of cycles until they reached the point of 
failure under conditions of rapid charging. The nominal capacity of 
the batteries was established at 1.1 Ah, and the nominal voltage at 3.3 
V. The EOL point was demarcated as the cycle number at which the 
state of health declined to 80%. All batteries were tested in a constant 
temperature environment of 30 ◦C and subjected to 72 different fast-
charging protocols. The charging process adopted a two-stage constant 
current strategy, denoted as ‘‘C1-C2’’, where C1 and C2 represent the 
constant current values in the first and second stages, respectively. The 
charging rates ranged from 3.6 C to 6 C (where 1C = 1.1 A), with 
3.6 C being the manufacturer-recommended fast-charging rate. As the 
state of charge attained 80%, the secondary charging phase concluded. 
This was succeeded by a 1C CC–CV charging phase, which persisted 
until the SOC reached 100%. During the charging process, the current 
gradually decreased to C/50 once the cut-off voltage was attained, at 
which point the charging cycle terminated. In contrast to the hetero-
geneity observed in charging strategies, the discharge process exhibited 
uniformity: all batteries were discharged at a constant current rate of 
4C, resulting in a decline to a voltage of 2.0 V. The operating voltage 
range of the batteries was set between 2.0 V and 3.6 V. The present 
study utilizes the ‘‘2017-06-30’’ batch of lithium-ion batteries from the 
dataset that was collectively established by the Massachusetts Institute 
of Technology and Stanford University. A total of 30 batteries with 
different charging protocols were randomly selected as experimental 
subjects, of which 24 were used as the training set and the remaining 
6 as the test set, designated as #1, #2, #3, #4, #5, and #6.
We also utilize the XJTU Li-ion battery dataset [59], which com-
prises two batches with distinct cycling protocols. Batch-1 consists of 8 
cells, and Batch-2 consists of 15 cells. For both batches, the first cycle 
serves as a capacity characterization step: a 0.5 C constant current–
constant voltage (CC–CV) charge to 4.2 V with a cutoff current of 0.02 
C (40 mA), a 5-min rest, and a 0.2 C (0.4 A) discharge to 2.5 V. In 
subsequent cycles, Batch-1 cells are charged at 2.0 C (4 A) with a 
CV cutoff of 0.05 C (0.1 A), rested for 5 min, and discharged at 1.0 
C (2 A) to 2.5 V, followed by a 5-min rest. Batch-2 employs a more 
aggressive 3.0 C (6 A) CC–CV charge with the same 0.05 C cutoff, while 
the discharge and rest conditions remain identical.

The hallmark of a truly trustworthy predictor is calibration, which 
is the alignment between the model’s predicted confidence and its 
actual accuracy. In order to validate the calibration of uncertainty esti-
mates, a further analysis was conducted to ascertain whether samples 
with high confidence, characterized by low uncertainty, indeed exhibit 
lower prediction errors. Specifically, the battery samples in the test set 
were initially sorted in ascending order based on the epistemic uncer-
tainty output by the model, where lower uncertainty indicates higher 
confidence. In a trustworthy system, selecting samples with higher 
confidence should strictly guarantee higher accuracy. Subsequently, 
a range of confidence thresholds were selected, representing the top 
x% of samples with the highest confidence. The average RMSE of the 
corresponding samples was then calculated. For instance, a confidence 
threshold of 0.05 corresponds to calculating the RMSE for the top 5% 
of samples with the lowest uncertainty.
In principle, if the uncertainty estimation of a method is accurate 
and well-calibrated, the RMSE should demonstrate a monotonically 
increasing trend as the confidence threshold expands from 0.05 to 0.95, 
gradually including more low-confidence samples. This is due to the 
fact that samples with lower prediction confidence, which corresponds 
to higher uncertainty, are generally more challenging for the model 
to predict accurately, thus resulting in larger prediction errors. As 
demonstrated in Fig.  15, the variation in average RMSE across different 
confidence quantiles for the six test cells is illustrated under the three 
methods.
The experimental results demonstrated that the deep evidential 
regression method exhibited a stable and significant increasing RMSE 
trend across all test cells, thus confirming its superior calibration 
characteristics in uncertainty estimation. Conversely, the MC Dropout 
method demonstrated anomalous behavior in specific cells (e.g., #3 and 
#4), where the RMSE in high-confidence intervals was unexpectedly 
higher than in low-confidence intervals. This finding suggests the pres-
ence of systematic bias in the estimation of uncertainty, which in turn 
indicates that the method may, on occasion, result in an overestimation 
of prediction confidence. This, in turn, gives rise to predictions that 
appear to be confident but which, in reality, contain a significant 
degree of error. Whilst the model ensemble method generally displayed 
an increasing RMSE trend in most cases, the magnitude of change 
remained limited, reflecting its restricted discriminative capability in 
uncertainty estimation and its difficulty in effectively distinguishing 
error differences between high and low-confidence samples.
Further analysis indicates that deep evidential regression performs 
remarkably well in identifying high-quality predictions. In high confi-
dence intervals (e.g., the top 5% and 25%), this method maintained the 
lowest RMSE values, thereby demonstrating its capacity to accurately 
identify the most trustworthy prediction results. This characteristic 
is of particular value in practical applications: well-calibrated uncer-
tainty estimates can provide battery management systems with a clear 
decision-making basis. High-confidence predictions can be directly used 
for maintenance planning, while high-uncertainty predictions should 
trigger further verification or conservative strategies.
In summary, the superior calibration of the proposed method trans-
forms RUL prediction from a mere numerical output into a trustworthy 
decision-support tool, enabling it to more reliably identify samples with 
larger prediction errors and thereby providing trustworthy support for 
predictive confidence assessment in RUL prediction tasks.
5. Conclusions
Accurate prediction combined with rigorous UQ creates the cor-
nerstone of trustworthy battery management in the energy storage 
industry. This paper proposes an SMoE-LSTM model, which advances 
both sequential pattern learning and UQ. The architecture employs 
a sparse gating mechanism within the collaborative fusion model of 
SMoE and LSTM to effectively capture temporal dynamics and complex 
degradation patterns from heterogeneous battery data. Importantly, 
this sparse gating mechanism provides structural explainability by 
enabling explicit tracing of expert activation patterns across different 
degradation stages, revealing how the model adaptively focuses its 
computational resources. Crucially, the integration of Deep Evidential 
Regression fundamentally enhances the model’s trustworthiness by 
enabling the simultaneous output of point predictions and decoupled 
uncertainty estimates. Experimental results on the MIT-Stanford dataset 
demonstrate the model’s effectiveness in achieving trustworthy accu-
racy. Our model achieves an average RMSE of 16.11 and an R2 of 
0.9821 across six test cells, while providing well-calibrated uncertainty 
estimates with an average Spearman correlation of 0.49. The single-
pass evidential inference further reduces prediction time to 0.04 s per
